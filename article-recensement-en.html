Gabon’s 2026 census: A critical analysis of the 3,518,621 population figure

Main finding

The certified figure calls for a documented explanation.

This analysis does not claim to establish a single cause. It examines what can be concluded from the information made public and identifies the data needed to resolve the question.

Executive summary

On August 27, 2026, Gabon’s Constitutional Court certified the results of the General Population and Housing Census (RGPL), setting the resident population at 3,518,621. The previous census, conducted in October 2013, put the population at 1,811,079. The new figure amounts to almost a doubling in 13 years.

Based on the data published to date, the certified figure cannot be reconciled with any plausible combination of births, deaths and migration. This is not a marginal disagreement over a margin of error, but a gap of a different order of magnitude that calls for a documented explanation. That explanation may already exist in the detailed tables the Central Census Bureau has not yet released: this report urges readers to verify that possibility first, rather than dismiss it.

Five findings

  1. An unprecedented rate in Gabon’s census series. The implied average annual population growth rate is 5.24%. The highest rate calculated from available Gabonese censuses is 2.98%, between 1970 and 1993. Sustaining growth above 5% for more than a decade is exceptional, usually associated with mass labour migration or an influx of refugees.
  2. A net migration balance with no documented precedent. Assuming natural increase of 2.35% per year — already a generous assumption derived from Gabonese surveys — net migration of about 1.07 million people over 13 years, or roughly 82,000 a year, would be required. Foreign nationals would then make up 41% of residents. By comparison, Côte d’Ivoire, a long-standing destination for migrants, recorded 22% in 2021, a share that has declined steadily since 1975.
  3. An unprecedented geographic concentration. Estuaire would grow from 895,689 to 2,184,908 residents: 62.1% of the national population and 75.5% of national growth over the period. Its density would rise from 43 to 105 people per square kilometre.
  4. A tension with the electoral register. In April 2025, 920,200 voters were registered. Compared with the 2026 population, that would imply a registration rate of around 56% to 62% among Gabonese adults, even though official turnout for the election was 87%. Reversing the calculation points to a total population in the range of 2.2 to 2.9 million.
  5. A large gap with international projections. The United Nations estimates Gabon’s population at 2,647,399 in mid-2026. The certified figure is 871,222 higher — a difference of 32.9%.

What this means in practice

Three explanations are possible and likely interact: the 2013 census may have undercounted the population; immigration may have been far greater than international organizations assumed; or the 2026 census may have overcounted. The report examines these explanations together. Even using generous assumptions — an 8% undercount in 2013 and 20,000 net migrants per year — an unexplained gap of about 550,000 people, nearly 19%, remains.

The issue is not symbolic. Every population-based indicator — GDP per capita, physician density, school enrolment rates and public debt per person — falls mechanically by 24.8% if the new figure replaces the UN estimate. Local government transfers, health and school planning, and eventually the allocation of parliamentary seats will rely on these counts.

Central recommendation

One measure would resolve most of the questions raised here: publish the detailed RGPL 2026 tables as soon as possible — breakdowns by age, sex and nationality, the number and size of households, and the number of dwellings. These data exist: collecting them is inherent to the census. Releasing them promptly would either validate the figure or identify the source of the gap, bringing the debate to a close within weeks.

This release should be complemented by a post-enumeration survey, as Côte d’Ivoire did before publishing its final results and as Canada has done since 1961.
