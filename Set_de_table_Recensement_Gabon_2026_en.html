The calculation

The equation that does not add up

Population changes in only four ways: births, deaths, people arriving and people leaving. This calculation applies natural increase of 2.35% per year, derived from surveys by Gabon’s own Directorate General of Statistics — the 2019–2021 EDSG-III measured 31.3 births per 1,000 people. This is a low estimate, and therefore one that favours the official figure.

To close this gap through immigration, Gabon would need 82,239 net arrivals every year for 13 years — nearly four times the country’s entire foreign-national population in 2013. The gap is not caused by a disagreement with outside sources; it arises within Gabon’s own data.

The nine provinces

A country tipping toward one corner

From 2013 to 2026, Estuaire would grow from 895,689 to 2,184,908 residents — 7.10% per year. The other eight provinces combined would grow by 2.94% per year: they did not lose population, so internal migration cannot account for Estuaire’s increase.

According to the Directorate General of Statistics survey, Estuaire has the country’s lowest fertility rate — 4.1 children per woman — while Ogooué-Ivindo has the highest, at 6.7. Yet Estuaire grows the fastest, while Ogooué-Lolo, at 5.3, grows the slowest. The correlation between fertility and population growth is negative.

Putting the rate in context

What does 5.24% a year look like?

Average annual population growth between two censuses. Rates above 5% over a decade are generally found only in Gulf petro-monarchies and countries receiving large numbers of refugees. Neighbouring Congo also nearly doubled its population, but at 3.2% per year.

How other countries do it

A good census does not claim to be exact. It publishes its error.

Canada

Three independent coverage studies since 1961. Published net undercount: 3.00%, with a margin of ±0.2 percentage points.

France

Continuous data collection spread over five years since 2004. Methods, weighting and adjustments are fully documented.

Côte d'Ivoire

A post-enumeration survey of a sample was conducted in 2022 before any final results were published.

Gabon

Consultations with governors, prefects and village chiefs. A 97% coverage rate was announced, but no adjustment was applied.

The State says so itself. The 2026 National Human Development Report, published by the Ministry of Planning and Foresight, describes the shortage of reliable statistical data in Gabon as “a major obstacle to evidence-based public policymaking.” The same report puts Gabon’s Human Development Index at 0.693: dividing income per capita by 1.33 would lower it by about 0.014 points.

Results are still being published. These figures come from the overall results certified on August 27, 2026. The Central Census Bureau has announced that more detailed analyses will follow. Several missing data points — age, nationality, households and dwellings — are likely to be released and could either confirm or challenge the total. What matters is their timely release, not the good faith of those who produced the figures.
