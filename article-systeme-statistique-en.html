Building an effective statistical system: legitimacy, credibility and independence

Gabon, 16 years later: from the Directorate General of Statistics to INSTAT. A 2026 update to the article published on October 30, 2010.

FBy Franklin Assoumou-Ndong, statistics analystOttawa, Ontario, Canada · August 30, 2026
Disclaimer: The information and opinions in this report are those of the author alone and do not represent an official position of Statistics Canada, INSTAT Gabon or any other organization cited.

The central point

Gabon has created the institution called for in 2010. The challenge now is to make INSTAT credible, independent and sustainable.

Executive summary

In 2010, the article made a simple argument: a statistical system cannot stand on technical competence alone. It depends on legitimacy — society’s recognition that statistics serve the public interest — and credibility — users’ trust in the people who produce figures they can almost never verify for themselves. It concluded that a directorate housed in a ministry could not meet these conditions and called for a national agency as independent as possible from political power.

The call was heard. Law No. 016/2022 of September 6, 2022, supplementing Law No. 015/2014, created the National Institute of Statistics (INSTAT Gabon). Its statutes were approved on June 20, 2024 and published in the Official Gazette in August 2024, alongside the creation of a National Statistics Council and a Special Statistics Fund. The institute’s leaders, appointed on October 23, 2025, were sworn in on December 5, 2025. The 2026 general census used fully digitized data collection. This is a major institutional advance and deserves unreserved recognition.

The challenge has shifted. The task is no longer to create the institution, but to make it credible, independent and sustainable. The report identifies three structural weaknesses:

  1. Appointment powers extend down to production teams. The director general, deputy, technical directors and even department heads are all appointed by decree of the Council of Ministers, with no protected fixed terms.
  2. Governance does not broadly represent data users. The board includes eight ministry representatives and only one scientific member — no businesses, universities, unions or media.
  3. Funding and the Special Fund raise questions about sustainability. Funding relies heavily on an external loan, while the Special Fund is managed outside the institute.

The credibility challenge

Available indicators confirm the scale of the remaining work. The World Bank rated Gabon’s statistical performance at 45.5 out of 100 in 2024. The latest published major social indicators still date from 2017, and the latest national accounts exercise shown is for 2022.

The institute’s own measure of user satisfaction is 50.7% in 2026: only one user in two.

The 2026 census is the first full-scale test. The 3,518,621 residents certified on August 27, 2026 represent a 94.3% increase since 2013 and exceed international estimates by roughly one million, yet the detailed methodological report has not been made public.

The literature is clear about what is at stake. The World Bank’s 2021 World Development Report, devoted to data, notes that statistical capacity and data literacy remain weak in low-income countries, and that data systems often exclude the most vulnerable. The work of Morten Jerven and Shanta Devarajan’s diagnosis of an “African statistical tragedy” show that a wrong figure is not an academic curiosity: it shapes budgets, aid and income classifications.

A technological leap — with safeguards

Artificial intelligence creates an opportunity for a technological leap. An institute being built today has no legacy systems to dismantle, and the georeferenced mapping produced through the 2026 census offers a necessary base layer. Published research shows that deep learning applied to open satellite imagery explains 70% of the variation in ground-measured wealth in African villages.

Responsible use is essential. The 2025 international framework for AI in official statistics and the practices published by Statistics Canada offer a doctrine ready to adapt. The central rule is simple: a human validates every result, and no official figure is produced by a generative model.

A two-stage roadmap

The report first proposes nine quick wins, achievable in less than a year at little or no cost:

  1. Publish the census’s full methodology.
  2. Follow an announced release calendar.
  3. Put founding legal texts online.
  4. Publish a revision policy.
  5. Release anonymized microdata.
  6. Include an explanatory note with every release.
  7. Publish the membership of the National Statistics Council.
  8. Hold quarterly briefings with the press.
  9. Adopt a public policy for the use of artificial intelligence.

It then proposes 10 structural priorities, including three decisive ones:

  1. Provide statutory protection for the director general’s term.
  2. Rebuild the foundation of household surveys.
  3. Develop a multi-year training plan supported by ISSEA in Yaoundé, a CEMAC institution co-owned by Gabon.
