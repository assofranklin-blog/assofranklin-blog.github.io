Can the unemployment rate published by INSTAT Gabon be considered reliable?

An assessment of ENEC 2024's 17.4% in light of International Labour Organization standards and the practices of leading statistical agencies, based on documents published by INSTAT Gabon.

People often say that African statistics are not worth much. The publications from the 2024 national employment survey are far better than that reputation suggests. They explain the sample design, publish margins of error by stratum, acknowledge their own limitations, and the summary report was reviewed by five International Labour Organization officials. The problem lies elsewhere — and it is serious.

Asking whether a rate is reliable is not asking whether it is “true.” No survey statistic is exact in an absolute sense. Reliability is the property of a figure whose production is documented, reproducible and auditable.

1What the rate measures — and what it does not

Under the International Labour Organization definition, a person is counted as unemployed only when all three conditions are met: they have no job, they are available to work, and they have actively looked for work recently. ENEC applies this definition and specifies the reference periods: no production activity in the past seven days, active job search during that period or the previous 30 days, and availability within 15 days at the latest.

A university graduate in Libreville who has stopped looking because they have lost hope is not counted as unemployed: they are classified as inactive. A street vendor who works two hours a day is not unemployed either: they are employed. The lived reality of underemployment is therefore largely missed by this indicator. That is not a measurement flaw; it is a limitation of the definition.

The unemployment rate must never be read on its own

A falling rate can mean two opposite things: unemployed people found jobs, or they gave up looking and left the labour force. Fortunately, ENEC publishes all three indicators together. Between 2010 and 2024, unemployment fell from 20.4% to 17.4%, while participation rose from 48.7% to 49.5% and the employment rate rose from 38.8% to 40.9%. The decline is therefore not explained by people leaving the labour force: it is the pattern of a real improvement.

Two figures deserve more public attention than 17.4%. The participation rate: fewer than half of working-age people take part in the labour market. And the informal-employment rate: 86.5%, meaning nearly nine in ten jobs are outside social security coverage.

2Eight conditions for reliability

Reliability is not declared; it is demonstrated. Eight conditions, drawn from ILO standards, the Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics and the practices of leading agencies, make it possible to assess it.

Standard — what definition?  ·  Source — where does the data come from?  ·  Sampling frame — which census?  ·  Precision — within how many points?  ·  Timeliness — how often, and with what delay?  ·  Transparency — can I reproduce the calculation?  ·  Accessibility — can the public find it?  ·  Independence — who decides when to publish?

Precision deserves particular attention: it determines not only what conclusions can be drawn, but the geographic level at which a figure can be published. It will play a decisive role in the assessment that follows.

3What leading agencies do

Canada measures unemployment through the Labour Force Survey, launched in November 1945 and conducted monthly since 1952 among about 65,000 households. Statistics Canada sets an explicit target: a coefficient of variation no higher than 2% nationally and 4% to 7% provincially. Results are released about ten days after data collection ends.

France conducts its survey quarterly, covering about 90,000 people aged 15 and over each quarter. With every release, it publishes the unemployment, activity and employment rates, the share of people in the unemployment halo, and the associated confidence interval — plus or minus 0.3 percentage points.

What these two systems have in common is not national wealth or sample size. It is the documentation surrounding the figure. Neither Statistics Canada nor INSEE asks the public to take its word for it: both provide the means to challenge their results. In this respect, ENEC 2024 belongs to the same tradition.

4What ENEC 2024 actually publishes

The operation was conducted jointly by the Directorate General of Employment and the Directorate General of Statistics, now INSTAT, with technical and financial support from the World Bank. Data collection took place in November and December 2024; results were presented on April 29, 2026.

In plain language, a coefficient of variation of 3.9% means the true value is very likely within a range of about one and a half percentage points of the published 17.4%. That is respectable precision for a survey of 3,408 households.

Four items are missing. None prevents readers from using the results, but all limit a third party's ability to audit them in depth. Neither questionnaire has been published — yet the precise wording of questions about job search and availability determines how people are classified as unemployed, inactive or part of the potential labour force. There is no anonymized microdata file or standardized metadata sheet, and the results do not yet appear to have been added to ILOSTAT.

5Three serious caveats

The figure is reliable only at the national level

Outside Libreville and Port-Gentil, the coefficients of variation for unemployment exceed 10%, and in seven of the 14 strata they exceed 16%. In the report's own words, this reflects low statistical precision in the corresponding provinces.

Two official population figures

This is the most serious difficulty. The survey weights its results to a population of 2,031,882 people, 62.1% of whom are aged 15 or older. On August 27, 2026, the Constitutional Court certified the results of the general census, which put the resident population at 3,518,621 people, with a reported coverage rate of 97%.

The objection is valid for the rate. It is not valid for the counts: if the actual population is close to 3.5 million, the number of unemployed people is not 108,733 but substantially higher, and all numerical targets in public policies built on those counts need to be revisited. Nor does it resolve the coverage issue: a 2013 sampling frame does not include residential areas created since then, and poorly represents a population that is now one-third foreign-born — precisely the group the survey shows behaves differently, with an unemployment rate of 5.2% compared with 22.1% for non-migrants.

Where are the discouraged workers?

The survey classifies 636,491 people as outside the labour force and identifies only 3,341 as potential labour force. The resulting expanded unemployment rate is 17.8%, compared with 17.4% under the strict definition — a difference of 0.4 percentage points. Senegal, with an equally informal economy, measures a gap nearly eighteen times larger: 5.1% under the strict definition versus 22.9% under the expanded one. In a country where labour-force participation is 49.5%, the claim that fewer than one in 190 people outside the labour force wants to work needs supporting evidence.

Why is the rate 17.4% when Senegal reports 5.1%?

The answer is structural. In a largely informal and rural economy, almost everyone does some work, and very few people meet all three unemployment criteria at once. Gabon is different: with an urbanization rate of 87.8%, measured by the survey itself, and subsistence agriculture that absorbs little labour, it does not have the same cushion of rural informal activity. Gabon's figure is therefore economically plausible, and is supported by a rural unemployment rate of 5.8%, close to levels seen in West Africa.

Within CEMAC, ENEC 2024 is currently the most recent, comprehensive and best-documented operation. Two caveats qualify this leading position: the regional bar is low, as no country in the area produces a quarterly indicator; and Gabon's lead rests on a one-off survey that will fade if the next edition takes just as long to arrive.

Outside CEMAC, the comparison changes. The World Bank classifies Gabon as an upper-middle-income country. Senegal, whose per-capita income is several times lower, publishes a full employment bulletin every quarter. The argument is therefore not regional but fiscal.

Yes, the national rate of 17.4% can be considered reliable: it is properly defined and measured, its precision is known, and its methodology is public. Provincial rates cannot, as the report itself states. And all the counts rest on a demographic baseline made obsolete by the 2026 census.

1. Publish the questionnaires
The most useful missing item: it would show how the line between unemployment, inactivity and potential labour force was drawn.

Two initiatives are already underway, and it would be unfair not to acknowledge them: in August 2026, INSTAT received a support mission to prepare the Harmonized Survey on Household Living Conditions, as well as technical assistance to establish the governing bodies of the national statistical system. Updating the sampling frame and clarifying governance are therefore not starting from zero.

Above all: the participation rate

The 49.5% participation rate is economically more significant than the unemployment rate, yet it went largely unnoticed. It should be broken down by sex, age and place of residence, and the available differences should be discussed: participation is higher in rural areas (57.7%) than in urban areas (48.4%), while international migrants have an unemployment rate of 5.2% compared with 22.1% for non-migrants. These differences call for targeted policies.

9Conclusion

An official figure is a promise to citizens: that, if they wish, they can verify how it was produced. ENEC 2024 keeps that promise in substance. It is serious work, and it deserves to be recognized as such.

Three tasks remain. The simplest is the audit trail: publish the questionnaires, metadata and an anonymized microdata file so Gabonese universities can work with the data rather than its summary. The most urgent is the demographic baseline. The most demanding is regularity: a country that can produce a survey of this quality once every 14 years can learn to produce one every quarter.

The national unemployment rate published by INSTAT Gabon can be considered reliable, provided it is read for what it is: a national estimate, known to within about one and a half percentage points, based on a demographic snapshot that has since been superseded by the 2026 census.
