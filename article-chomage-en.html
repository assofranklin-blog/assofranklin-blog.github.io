Is Gabon’s unemployment rate published by INSTAT reliable?

People often say that African statistics are not worth much. I read Gabon’s line by line — the publications from its 2024 national employment survey. They are far better than their reputation suggests, and the real problem lies elsewhere.

F By Franklin Assoumou-Ndong, statistics analystOttawa, September 12, 2026 · This analysis was produced in a personal capacity, based solely on public sources. It does not represent any employer or partner organization.

The verdict in three points

Yes, at the national level.

No, at the provincial level.

And all published counts — the number of unemployed people, the labour force and the working-age population — rely on a demographic baseline that has since been superseded by the 2026 census.

Why the question deserves a rigorous answer

Three or four figures are enough to sum up a country’s economic health in the eyes of the world: growth, inflation, public debt and unemployment. They circulate in International Monetary Fund reports, sovereign ratings and investment briefs. They end up having real effects, regardless of their accuracy.

Unemployment holds a particular place in that list: it is the one that speaks directly to people’s lives. When a government says unemployment is falling, it is implicitly asking its citizens to believe it.

One distinction needs to be made. Asking whether a rate is reliable is not asking whether it is “true.” No survey statistic is exact in an absolute sense: it is an estimate with uncertainty, and a statistician’s job is precisely to measure and disclose that uncertainty. Reliability is the property of a figure whose production is documented, reproducible and auditable.

What the unemployment rate measures — and what it does not

Under the International Labour Organization definition, a person is counted as unemployed only when all three conditions are met: they have no job, they are available to work, and they have actively looked for work recently. Gabon’s survey applies that definition and specifies its reference periods: no production activity in the past seven days, active job search during that period or the previous 30 days, and availability within 15 days at the latest.

Every working-age person is assigned to one category, and one category only.

Because all three conditions must be met, the indicator can seem counterintuitive. A university graduate in Libreville who has stopped looking because they have lost hope is not counted as unemployed: they are classified as inactive. A street vendor who works two hours a day is not unemployed either: they are employed.

The unemployment rate must never be read on its own

A falling rate can mean two opposite things: unemployed people found jobs, or they gave up looking and left the labour force. In the second case, conditions have worsened even as the figure improves.

Fortunately, Gabon’s survey publishes all three indicators together, and reading them jointly is reassuring. Between 2010 and 2024, unemployment fell from 20.4% to 17.4%, while labour-force participation rose from 48.7% to 49.5% and the employment rate rose from 38.8% to 40.9%. The decline in unemployment is therefore not explained by people leaving the labour force: participation and employment rose at the same time. That is the pattern of a real improvement.

Two figures deserve more public attention than 17.4%. The first is the participation rate: fewer than half of working-age people take part in the labour market. The second is the informal-employment rate, at 86.5%: nearly nine in ten jobs are not covered by social security.

What the survey actually publishes

The operation was conducted jointly by the Directorate General of Employment and the Directorate General of Statistics, now INSTAT, with technical and financial support from the World Bank. Data collection took place in November and December 2024; the results were presented on April 29, 2026.

ElementWhat the document contains
DefinitionStrict ILO standard, with the precise reference periods stated
Conceptual framework19th ICLS, including all four labour-underutilization indicators, LU1 to LU4
Sample design22 strata, 176 enumeration areas, two-stage sampling, 3,520 households selected, 3,408 retained, 96.8% response rate
PrecisionCoefficients of variation calculated and published by stratum for four indicators
LimitationsThe report itself states that provincial unemployment rates have low precision
ReviewReview committee of five International Labour Organization officials in Geneva, Abidjan and Dakar

This last point deserves emphasis. At the national level, the coefficients of variation are 1.2% for participation, 1.5% for employment and 3.9% for unemployment. In plain language, a 3.9% coefficient means the true value is very likely within about one and a half percentage points of the published 17.4%. That is respectable precision for a survey of 3,408 households.

First caveat: the figure is reliable only nationally

The survey does more than publish its precision: it draws conclusions from it and says so. Outside Libreville and Port-Gentil, coefficients of variation for unemployment exceed 10%, and in seven of the 14 strata they exceed 16%. In the report’s own words, this indicates low statistical precision for the corresponding provincial rates. Few statistical agencies say this about their own work.

Above 16%, the report itself considers precision low.

Yet the practical consequence is not flagged in the tables. The presentation does not distinguish the national rate of 17.4% from the Nyanga rate, whose estimate has a 33.3% coefficient of variation. A provincial rate this imprecise cannot be used to rank provinces, track change or target an employment program. The warning exists, but it is buried in a methodological subsection few readers will reach.

Second caveat: two official population figures

This is the most serious difficulty. The survey’s summary table begins with a decisive figure: its estimated population is 2,031,882, of whom 62.1% are aged 15 and over, with an urbanization rate of 87.8%. That age structure is entirely plausible.

On August 27, 2026, following field checks, the Constitutional Court certified the general census results, putting the resident population at 3,518,621. The census was conducted entirely digitally, with geolocation, and a national coverage rate of 97% was reported.

The survey weights its results to a population of 2.03 million; the census certified in August counts 3.52 million.

The gap is nearly 1.49 million people. A formal reconciliation is not yet possible: the census age structure has not been released, nor have the departmental breakdown or methodology needed to reconstruct how collected data became the certified total.

An informed reader may object that unemployment is a ratio and a change in population scale does not mechanically affect it. That is true of the rate. It is not true of the counts: if the actual population is close to 3.5 million, the number of unemployed people is not 108,733 but substantially higher, and every numerical target in public policy built on those counts needs to be revisited.

Third caveat: where are the discouraged workers?

The survey classifies 636,491 people as outside the labour force and counts just 3,341 as potential labour force — people without work who do not meet every unemployment criterion but would nevertheless like to work.

This result deserves scrutiny. It implies a broad unemployment rate of 17.8%, compared with 17.4% under the strict definition, a gap of just 0.4 percentage points. Senegal, with an economy also marked by informality, records a gap of nearly 18 points between its two definitions: 5.1% under the strict definition versus 22.9% under the broad one. Gabon’s labour market may genuinely be different; it is also possible that the questions on availability and desire for work were applied narrowly. In a country where labour-force participation is 49.5%, the claim that fewer than one in 190 inactive people wants to work calls for supporting evidence.

Why 17.4% when Senegal reports 5.1%?

The question comes up often, and the answer is structural. Under the strict ILO definition, Senegal reports 5.1% and Côte d’Ivoire 2.3%. These very low rates do not mean full employment: in largely informal, rural economies, almost everyone does some work, and very few people meet all three unemployment criteria at once.

The definition used, as much as the structure of the labour market, explains the difference between the figures.

Gabon is different. With an urbanization rate of 87.8%, measured by the survey itself, and little subsistence agriculture to absorb labour, the country lacks the cushion of rural informal activity that mechanically pushes down strict unemployment elsewhere. Gabon’s figure is therefore economically coherent — and is supported by a rural unemployment rate of 5.8%, close to West African levels.

Gabon compared with its CEMAC peers

A comparison is only useful when the reference group makes sense. The first circle is CEMAC: shared regional institutions, similar technical support and comparable constraints.

The finding favours Gabon: within CEMAC, its 2024 survey is currently the most recent, comprehensive and well-documented operation, and Gabon is the only country in the region to have published coefficients of variation by stratum in 2026.

Two caveats qualify this leading position. The regional bar is low: no CEMAC country currently produces a quarterly labour-market indicator. And Gabon’s lead rests on a one-off survey; it will disappear if the next edition takes as long to arrive as this one did.

Outside CEMAC, the comparison changes. The World Bank classifies Gabon as an upper-middle-income country. Senegal, whose per-capita income is several times lower, publishes a full employment bulletin every quarter. The argument is therefore not regional but fiscal: the constraint preventing Gabon from doing the same is one of organization and prioritization, not funding.

Six measures that cost almost nothing

  1. Publish both questionnaires. This is the most useful missing piece: it would let readers verify how the line between unemployment, inactivity and potential labour force was drawn.
  2. Flag precision in the tables. Add a coefficient-of-variation column or a quality symbol, and an explicit warning beside fragile estimates.
  3. Reconcile the two population figures. Publish a note explaining the change from 2,031,882 to 3,518,621 people and the resulting revision to counts.
  4. Publish standardized metadata describing the operation, its definitions and its limitations.
  5. Bridge 2010 and 2024. International standards changed in 2013 between the two surveys; a methodological note would make the comparison more robust.
  6. Submit the results to ILOSTAT. Until they are, international comparisons for Gabon will continue to rely on a modelled estimate rather than a national measurement.

Two initiatives are already underway, and it would be unfair not to acknowledge them: in August 2026, INSTAT received a support mission to prepare the Harmonized Survey on Household Living Conditions and technical assistance to establish the governing bodies of the national statistical system. Updating the sampling frame and clarifying statistical governance are not starting from zero.

Conclusion

An official figure is a promise to citizens: that if they wish, they can verify how it was produced. The 2024 survey keeps that promise in substance. It explains its sample design, publishes coefficients of variation by stratum, states its own limitations, uses the current international conceptual framework and submitted its report for review by the International Labour Organization. This is serious work, and it deserves recognition.

Three tasks remain. The simplest is the audit trail: publish the questionnaires, metadata and an anonymized microdata file so Gabonese universities can work with the data instead of its summary. The most urgent is the demographic baseline. The most demanding is regularity: a country that can produce a survey of this quality once every 14 years can learn to produce one every quarter.

“A statistic earns authority only when it is willing to be challenged. This one has made that possible, and that is already a great deal.”
